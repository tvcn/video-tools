Real Estate & Space Structure: Six Beats

First decision: when to use it

Real-estate video is not a wide-angle building tour. Start with the life problem of who lives here, then prove it with space; every shot answers one living question.

Use it when there is one clear subject, one action, and a visible change in the final film. Do not use it when there is only a slogan, no person, and no process to shoot; go back to the brief before rolling.

Case: a 90-second property film built on one living problem

What happened

  1. The first cut pushed a wide-angle from the doorway. The space looked big but said nothing. We started with the bag, keys and parcel in hand after entering.
  2. The camera followed one route: entry, kitchen, balcony. Each space answered one functional question.
  3. The ending held on window light and a chair, not the building exterior or sales office.

The trade-off

We dropped ultra-wide and “penthouse” moves for 35mm and a resident's point of view. Mixed interior light was harsh, so ceiling lights went off, window light plus a bounce became the source. The image did not chase brightness; it chased material and route.

Reusable / not transferable

  • Reusable: start with a living problem, let the space answer; one function per space.
  • Not transferable: shooting with the owner present needs a clear-out and release. Do not film private belongings or a neighbor's door for “real life.”

The three most common failures in this format

  1. Ultra-wide establishing shots. The space looks big but not livable. Lead each room with one living problem.
  2. No route. The camera jumps around; viewers do not know how rooms connect. Follow one real path.
  3. All lights on. Ceiling light flattens material and depth. Turn it off, use window light and a bounce, keep shadow.

Ask: which room solves which problem? If you cannot answer, it is property data, not a spatial film.

Six beats: one job per beat

Write the six beats as six sentences, each answering “what does the viewer know now.” Shoot in order, but cut out of order; do not add empty shots to fill the structure.

  1. Doorway problem
  2. Spatial route
  3. One function
  4. One detail
  5. Light/time
  6. Leaving

How to shoot it: six tasks

At least one primary shot per beat; do not shoot everything in one framing and rely on jump cuts. Shoot irreplaceable action first—hands, face, machine—then pickups.

  • Doorway: keys/shoes/parcel
  • Route: door to window in one move
  • Function: one action proves it (cook/storage)
  • Detail: hand/material/edge
  • Light: same room morning/evening
  • Leaving: lights off/door closes, one question left

Common failures: three ways it collapses

1. Logo/drone first: the viewer does not know whose story this is. Replace beat one with a problem or action. 2. Narration in every beat: the images carry no information. Let one action speak. 3. Unverifiable result: “more efficient” with no before/after or third party. Shoot something checkable.

Cost and check: can it be shot in a day

Typically one shoot day plus two to three post days: half a day for primary shots, half for pickups; cut structure first, then sound. Check only three things: six beats complete, one primary shot per beat, result checkable. Do not accept “it feels premium.”

6 checks

  • Each of six beats is one clear sentence.
  • Beat one is a problem/action, not a logo.
  • At least one primary shot per beat.
  • Result has before/after or third party.
  • At least one irreplaceable action captured.
  • Six beats survive the rough cut.
