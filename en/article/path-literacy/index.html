Media Literacy

Judge a video in a fixed order: source, edit, context, money, AI traces, charts, rights. Pause before concluding.

Pick three recent videos: one that made you angry, one that moved you, one you thought was fake. Do not verify yet; write your first reaction.

How the path runs

Media literacy is not “believe nothing”; it is a repeatable order: source, edit, context, then money and rights.

  1. Step 1: Source: Start from the earliest version, not the share chain. Check account history, first post time, cross-platform versions, and visual geography. Find at least two pieces of evidence before naming who. If there is no first post, write “source unknown”; do not fill the gap. Output: A source table: account, time, first link.
  2. Step 2: Edit: The emotional peak is often the densest edit. Look for action cuts, sound cuts, and reaction shots: which step is missing between two shots? Reverse the order and test whether the conclusion still holds. Output: Three cuts and three “what was removed.”
  3. Step 3: Context: Three common cuts: head cut, tail cut, reframing. Return to the original, restore the lines before and after, then check title, cover, and music. Context decides whether a line is a statement, a joke, or irony. Output: Original context and a title/music change table.
  4. Step 4: Money: Look for ad labels, sponsorship, affiliate links, paid promotion, and account ownership. No label does not mean no commercial relationship. Money does not automatically make content false, but it explains why it appears now and why it says this. Output: A money-relationship map.
  5. Step 5: AI traces: A trace shows a tool failed; it does not settle truth alone. Log all five checks, then weigh the source. A generated video can tell a true story; live footage can be edited into a lie. Output: A five-point check table.
  6. Step 6: Charts: Does the axis start at zero? Who is the sample? Which period was chosen? What is the denominator? Correlation is not causation. Charts lie less through numbers than through what is left out. Output: Four chart checks.
  7. Step 7: Rights: “Downloadable” is not “commercial use.” Music, fonts, footage, likeness, and location: missing one can take a film down. For your own work, keep releases in the project folder; for others, ask whether rights are cleared. Output: A five-item rights list.

Three exercises

  1. Exercise 1: Source table for one video: Find first post, account history, cross-platform versions, visual geography; mark unknown if missing. Output: A source table.
  2. Exercise 2: Reverse the order: Take a causal video, swap two shots, and test the conclusion. Output: A two-hundred-character analysis.
  3. Exercise 3: Five-item rights list: List music, font, footage, likeness, and location for your next film. Output: An actionable list.

Deliverables

  • A source table
  • Three cut analyses
  • Original context
  • A money map
  • A five-point AI check
  • Four chart checks
  • A five-item rights list

Where it goes wrong

  • Treating an AI artifact as proof of a fake: it shows a tool failed, not what happened.
  • Concluding from one source: use two independent sources or mark unknown.
  • Treating “downloadable” as “commercial”: rights are five items, not one image.

Next step

Next, take path-history to see where the technology came from, or read platform rules, copyright, and ethics on the context site.

Check

  • Find the first post before commenting.
  • Name the step a cut removed.
  • Restore at least one truncated context.
  • Name a beneficiary or write “not found.”
  • An AI trace is not a truth verdict by itself.
  • Chart check finds at least one omission.
  • All five rights are yes/no/unknown.
