Buying an HDR Monitor: Peak Nits Is Not Everything

HDR monitoring depends on peak, black level, gamut, tone mapping, and calibration. Buying on “1000 nits” alone gets a bright screen with clipped highlights.

First decision: which link is missing

Decide whether you deliver HDR or only view HDR references. For reference only, a calibrated SDR screen with a transform LUT can work. For an HDR master, peak, black, gamut, and tone mapping must all pass. Do not use a TV as a grading monitor; its sharpening and dynamic contrast lie.

Fits:

  • HDR master/grading
  • Platform HDR delivery QC
  • PQ/HLG reference

Does not fit:

  • SDR-only delivery
  • Under ¥10k, do SDR first
  • Viewing only, no grading

Test protocol: reproducible, no invented scores

Bring an HDR clip with a window, neon, and a dark scene. Use a signal generator to confirm PQ/HLG. Check whether blacks go grey and highlights clip to white. Use a color chart for P3 coverage.

Buying steps: bring footage, not spec sheets

Run these in order before paying. If one fails, do not tell yourself post will fix it.

  1. Bring HDR test clip
  2. Signal generator PQ/HLG
  3. Check black level
  4. Color chart P3
  5. Check calibration report/LUT

Check

  • Highlights have layers, not clipped white.
  • Dark scenes are not grey.
  • P3 gamut is acceptable.
  • PQ/HLG switch correct.
  • 3D LUT/calibration report exists.
  • Room light is controllable.

Deep dive

About 7 minutes. This section does not repeat the method above. It adds three things: two conditions, a counterexample and a reusable model, so you can decide when to change the method.

Condition A: mostly SDR

If ninety percent of the work is SDR, an HDR monitor is not the first priority. SDR needs accurate black, stable white and a reliable waveform; a 1000-nit HDR monitor with a bad SDR preset is worse. Check whether the SDR preset can be calibrated.

SDR monitoring also depends on ambient light: a bright room forces brightness up and black drifts. Decide shading and room level before buying; without environment control, an expensive monitor is still guesswork.

Condition B: HDR delivery

HDR delivery starts with who watches: PQ or HLG, what peak, whether Dolby Vision is required. The monitor must display the target peak and tone-map reliably; “1000 nits” is not enough, check mapping at 100, 400 and 1000 nits.

HDR also depends on shadows: it is not only brightness; black and near-black separation matter more. Take your own dark footage to test, not the showroom demo; demos are all highlights. If shadows collapse, HDR is only bright.

Counterexample: buying on peak nits alone

A monitor claims 1600 nits. At home everything below 100 nits is black, shadows have no layers; HDR highlights are bright but faces in shadow are a patch. The spec wins, the image loses.

The fix is not a brighter monitor; test three bands with your footage: shadow, mid, highlight. If each band shows layers, it is HDR monitoring. Peak is a ceiling, not a capability.

Reusable model

Delivery target → peak → tone mapping → shadows → environment. Set delivery, then peak, then mapping, then shadows, then room. With all five, an HDR monitor is not a spec ornament.

Before buying, run a three-band test: same footage at 100, 400 and 1000 nits, check shadow, skin and highlight. Only pay when all three are acceptable.
