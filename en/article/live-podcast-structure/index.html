Live & Podcast Structure: Six Beats

First decision: when to use it

Live/podcast is not uploading a long video. Design cuttable units: one complete question every 3–5 minutes so a viewer entering late still understands.

Use it when there is one clear subject, one action, and a visible change in the final film. Do not use it when there is only a slogan, no person, and no process to shoot; go back to the brief before rolling.

Case: a 40-minute live podcast with a complete question every four minutes

What happened

  1. The first 30 seconds had no introductions. It opened with one concrete question: “Why do you always cut until the last minute?” A late joiner could enter immediately.
  2. Every four minutes closed one question with a small conclusion. It did not depend on earlier jokes or assume the viewer had been there from the start.
  3. At minute 32 the audio dropped for 40 seconds. The operator did not stop the stream; we switched to the backup mic and kept going. The failure and recovery stayed in the cut.

The trade-off

We dropped the “complete course” monologue for a conversation you can enter at any point. Each block answers one question and repeats the key sentence, not the whole block. With no edit, pace, pauses and verbal habits stay; structure does the work, not post.

Reusable / not transferable

  • Reusable: give a complete question every 3–5 minutes; the first 30 seconds carry today's hook, not a CV.
  • Not transferable: two experienced hosts can go live without an edit. Guests or sensitive topics need delay, captions and a pull-back plan; “it's live” is not a safety net.

The three most common failures in this format

  1. Opening with a CV. Viewers do not know what today is about. Give a question or conflict in the first 30 seconds.
  2. Closing every ten minutes. Late joiners are lost. Give a complete question and small conclusion every 3–5 minutes.
  3. No backup plan. Drops, mic failure and dead air happen. Prepare a backup mic, delay and filler.

Start watching at minute 20: can you still follow? If yes, the segments work. If not, reorder the questions.

Six beats: one job per beat

Write the six beats as six sentences, each answering “what does the viewer know now.” Shoot in order, but cut out of order; do not add empty shots to fill the structure.

  1. Opening hook
  2. One question
  3. One story
  4. One counterexample
  5. One conclusion
  6. Next-episode hook

How to shoot it: six tasks

At least one primary shot per beat; do not shoot everything in one framing and rely on jump cuts. Shoot irreplaceable action first—hands, face, machine—then pickups.

  • Opening: question first, no intro
  • Question: two-shot, keep reactions
  • Story: cut to object/hands
  • Counterexample: change angle/size
  • Conclusion: close-up, one line
  • Next: leave one unanswered question

Common failures: three ways it collapses

1. Logo/drone first: the viewer does not know whose story this is. Replace beat one with a problem or action. 2. Narration in every beat: the images carry no information. Let one action speak. 3. Unverifiable result: “more efficient” with no before/after or third party. Shoot something checkable.

Cost and check: can it be shot in a day

Typically one shoot day plus two to three post days: half a day for primary shots, half for pickups; cut structure first, then sound. Check only three things: six beats complete, one primary shot per beat, result checkable. Do not accept “it feels premium.”

6 checks

  • Each of six beats is one clear sentence.
  • Beat one is a problem/action, not a logo.
  • At least one primary shot per beat.
  • Result has before/after or third party.
  • At least one irreplaceable action captured.
  • Six beats survive the rough cut.
