The Client Keeps Revising

Write the objective in one sentence and test every revision against it.

When the client says “one more version,” ask which problem it solves: pacing, information or emotion. Then offer two options with their costs. Let them choose rather than redefine at the next meeting.

Turn revisions into options, not infinity

The contract states revision rounds and scope per round; new requests outside scope get a separate quote.

Limits

Do not hide scope creep behind “the client is always right.” Professionalism is showing the cost of each choice, not unlimited compliance.

Write revisions as enforceable terms, not feelings

When a client says “it doesn't feel right,” it is usually not taste. It is the absence of a shared test. Revision terms must answer three things: what changes, how many rounds, and when it is done.

After two rounds, quote new work

The contract includes two revision rounds; round three bills by hour or extra edit day. This is not punishment; it makes cost visible. If round three introduces a structural change, stop, rewrite the script, then decide whether to continue.

Notes need a timecode and an action

Do not accept “make it more premium.” Ask for “00:12 subtitle is too fast, hold two seconds” or “00:20 music is too full, bring it to -18 LUFS.” One note solves one point, so nobody guesses.

Three signals of scope creep

  • “By the way” requests: an extra product close-up, an English version, a BTS cut.
  • The decision-maker changes: the original contact no longer approves; a new leader restarts notes.
  • Deliverables multiply: one film becomes three ratios, two languages and a social cut.

When any appears, send a one-page change order: new scope, new schedule, new cost. Start only after written approval.

On set

Write the goal in one sentence and check every revision against it. Quote separately for scope creep.

Do this

Endless client revisions: write the goal in one sentence, then judge every change against it.

  1. Put one line in the brief: “this film makes whom do what,” and get both sides to confirm.
  2. Collect notes in rounds, one category per round; quote extra rounds beyond the contract.
  3. After each change, ask whether it makes the goal clearer or fuzzier.

Avoid

Do not accept every note under “the client is always right”; unlimited revisions sink the project.

Boundary

This page only covers “The Client Keeps Revising”; it does not replace pricing, contract review, or the creative brief. If the problem belongs to another page in the related list, use that page first.

Alternative

If “Put one line in the brief: “this film makes whom do what,” and get both sides to confirm” is not the main problem, start with “Collect notes in rounds, one category per round; quote extra rounds beyond the contract”; only upgrade after both hold.

60-second test

Run one 60-second test against check 1: The brief contains one line: “makes whom do what,” confirmed by both sides. If check 2 (Notes arrive in rounds, one category per round) does not change, do not add gear or budget.

Check

  • The brief contains one line: “makes whom do what,” confirmed by both sides.
  • Notes arrive in rounds, one category per round.
  • Extra rounds are quoted; revisions are not unlimited.

Deep dive

About 6 minutes. Endless revisions are usually an unlocked brief, not a bad client. Turn spoken opinions into one page.

Lock the brief

Write five things: who watches, what they do after, what must appear, what must never appear, three reference films. Get confirmation on that page, not a “sure” in a chat thread.

Mark each reference “structure” or “style.” Without that, the client wants every strength of all three, and nobody can deliver it.

Three kinds of changes

Bug fixes are free: a typo, a black frame, out-of-sync audio. Scope changes are billed: a new scene, a new version, a different actor. Taste changes use the included round: color, music, pacing. Track the three separately so the client sees what they are asking for.

Give every revision a version number and a cutoff. Past the cutoff, it moves to the next round. Do not edit while chatting; otherwise nobody knows which version is current.

Protect the edit

Do not let clients into the timeline. Have them comment on a timecoded review link. Each note says minute, second, what to change, and why. “It feels off” is not a note.

If notes contradict each other, go back to the brief: which goal changed? If the goal is the same, keep the original plan. If the goal changed, it is a new project; quote again.

Five-minute drill: translate three notes

Take three client lines and translate each into one executable action: add a close-up, change the music to no drums, move the ending five seconds earlier. If you cannot translate it, book ten minutes on the phone; do not guess.

Put the three translations on the change sheet and ask the client to confirm “this is what I mean.” One confirmation can save three rounds.

When a revision is finished

A revision is done when the client confirms the latest version is deliverable, every note on the change sheet has an answer, and the version number stops moving. Do not end a round with “should be fine.”

Archive the final version with the change sheet. When someone later asks why this changed, the sheet answers. Unrecorded changes become new arguments.

Ten client-revision checks

  • Brief has five items
  • Client confirms on paper
  • References marked structure or style
  • Bug fixes free
  • Scope changes quoted
  • Taste changes use the round
  • Every revision numbered
  • Clear cutoff time
  • Timecoded review link
  • Contradictions return to the brief

Revisions are not a fight. They are proof that two people still lack the same one-page answer to “what is this film.”
