Producing & Management

Producing is not ordering lunch; it locks scope, money, people, time, permits, and risk into one table, and knows which overrun stops the shoot.

Bring a real project with cast, location, gear, budget, and a delivery date. If none, use a film you will actually shoot in the next three months, not a hypothetical.

Before you start

How the path runs

Producing works backward: define the stop line, build the schedule backward, then fill the budget. Do not start with a gear list and discover the money is short.

  1. Step 1: Scope: Three columns: deliverables, non-deliverables, revisions. Put “one more cut” and “one more subtitle language” under out of scope or a separate quote. Scope is a contract exhibit, not a wish list. Output: A one-page scope sheet.
  2. Step 2: Budget: Each block has a cap and cannot borrow from another. Contingency sits before the shoot, not at the end. The budget must answer “if one day disappears, which block is cut?” If not, it is numbers, not a decision tool. Rights, insurance, and transport get their own rows. Output: A five-block budget and one “cut first” answer.
  3. Step 3: Schedule: Lock delivery, then reserve thirty percent for post, forty for shoot, thirty for prep. Shoot the same location on the same day; shoot difficult light and children first. Leave a pickup window each day. Put a latest-decision date for cast, location, and gear. Output: A reverse schedule and three latest-decision dates.
  4. Step 4: People: Director, camera, sound, light, producing, post: who does it, who backs up, who to call. Do not write “we'll figure it out.” A key role without a backup is project risk. Output: A crew table: role/primary/backup/contact.
  5. Step 5: Permits and risk: Ask for permits for public space, shops, transit, and parks; confirm insurance covers gear and third parties. Risk sheet: who can get hurt, what can fall, who owns it, nearest hospital. The whole crew reads it; producing does not sign alone. Output: Permit list, risk sheet, nearest hospital.
  6. Step 6: Shoot day: Send the call sheet a day early with time, address, contact, parking, meals, hospital, load-out. Ten minutes before roll, the crew names the three must-get shots. Before wrap, confirm backup, log, prop return, and tomorrow's call sheet. Output: Daily call sheet, three must-get shots, wrap checklist.
  7. Step 7: Delivery and close: Lock one master with resolution, bitrate, captions, loudness, and filename. Archive releases by project for at least three years. Close with a review: where the budget overran, where the schedule failed, what changes next time. Output: Master, delivery sheet, rights archive, review.

Three exercises

  1. Exercise 1: One-page scope: Write deliverables/non-deliverables/revisions; show it to someone outside the project. Output: A one-page scope.
  2. Exercise 2: Reverse schedule: Build fourteen days backward from delivery and mark three latest-decision dates. Output: A reverse schedule.
  3. Exercise 3: Risk sheet: List weather, gear, people, and rights risks with one owner each. Output: A four-row risk sheet.

Deliverables

  • One-page scope
  • Five-block budget
  • Reverse schedule and three latest-decision dates
  • Crew table with backups
  • Permit list and risk sheet
  • Daily call sheet and wrap checklist
  • Master, delivery sheet, rights archive, review

Where it goes wrong

  • Starting with a gear list: gear is a result, not a starting point; lock scope and delivery first.
  • Expanding scope with “we'll do it first”: if money and time do not change, the project ends badly.
  • A risk sheet only producing reads: if the crew does not read it, it does not exist.

3 checks

  • Scope, money, people, and time share one sheet and cross-reference.
  • Weekly reviews are logged and changes go on change orders.
  • The last scope increase also adjusted money or time.
