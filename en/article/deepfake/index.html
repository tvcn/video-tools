Deepfake Detection: Look Beyond the Face

Look beyond the face: teeth, ears, blinks, background text, and light direction.

Check physics before the face

Deepfakes usually fail on physics, not features: mismatched shadow direction, wrong glasses reflection, changing tooth count, hair passing through a shoulder, a neck that does not move with speech. Frame-by-frame relationships are more reliable than “does it look like them.”

Then check provenance: where is the original, who shot it, is there an uncompressed copy? Without a source chain, the eye alone cannot conclude.

Limits

Do not treat an AI artifact as proof of a fake. Compression, beauty filters and low light create similar flaws. State probability and evidence, not “obviously fake.”

On set

Check teeth, ears, blinks, background text, and light direction. Watch five seconds frame by frame for anomalies.

Do this

Deepfakes are not only about the face. Check teeth, ears, blinking, background text, and light direction.

  1. Step through five seconds: are teeth a single row, ears symmetric, blinks natural?
  2. Check background text, signs, and reflections in glasses for continuity.
  3. Find the original source, a response from the person, or a second angle; if all three fail, treat it as high risk.

Avoid

Do not conclude “it looks fake”; name the specific mismatches.

Go deeper: for people already shooting

A deepfake is not “looks fake.” It is a mismatch across source, time, place, and body detail.

One step past the field note

Source first, pixels second

Find the original account, original stream, full version. An “exclusive” with no source is unknown. A fake fears the source; it does not fear your zoom.

Ask who is motivated to make it, when it was released, and what it wants you to share. Three questions arrive before any detection tool.

Where bodies break

Check teeth for a single row, ears for left-right symmetry, fingers for extra joints, blink rate for sudden changes, neck and collar shadows for consistency. AI fails at edges and contact points.

Check lip sync on plosives and pauses, then background lettering, signs, reflections in glasses. The tell is usually in the corner you ignore.

What to do after detection

Detection gives probability, not a verdict. Do not publicly accuse someone on a “97% fake” score. Find the original source, the person's response, and a second independent angle.

If you cannot confirm, report that the video is of unknown origin and unverified. Do not spread the details or turn the person into a joke. The victim is not material.

Five-minute drill: four checks on one video

Take one suspicious video. Check source, time, place, body detail. Write one line each: present, absent, uncertain. Do not write “feels fake.”

Show the four lines to someone and let them judge independently. If you disagree, recheck the weakest item; do not argue.

When you can conclude

You can say “highly likely fake” when at least three of four hold: no original source, time or place mismatch, hard body-detail failures, denial by the person or an authoritative party.

One item: write “questionable.” None: say nothing. A public accusation always costs more than ten more minutes of checking.

Ten deepfake checks

  • Find original account and stream
  • Check release time and motive
  • Teeth in one row
  • Ears symmetric
  • Finger joints normal
  • Blink rate not sudden
  • Neck and collar shadows consistent
  • Lip sync on plosives
  • Background text and glasses reflections
  • Three items before “highly likely”

Detecting a fake does not end at “I can tell.” It ends at “I can name the four mismatches.”
