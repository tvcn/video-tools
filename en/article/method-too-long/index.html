The Film Is Too Long

Find repetition: if one idea appears twice, cut the weaker instance.

Watch the whole cut without looking at the timeline, holding only a sheet of paper. Every time you reach for your phone or drift, note the time. See which scene those points share; cut that scene before changing speed.

Is the story long or the edit long?

  • Story: the goal arrives late or there are too many subplots. Remove a subplot before speeding anything up.
  • Edit: every shot holds two seconds too long, or transitions repeat. Trim six frames from both ends of each shot and watch again.
  • Structure: the same information appears twice. Merge the scenes and keep the more specific one.

When not to cut shorter

If removing it breaks the character's motivation, the problem is structure, not length. Add an action or a line instead of forcing the platform runtime.

On set

Find repeated information and cut the weaker one. One change per scene.

Do this

A long cut: find repetition. If the same information appears twice, keep the stronger version.

  1. Label what new information each scene provides; cut the scenes that provide none.
  2. Merge characters and locations that do the same job.
  3. Run a test screening, note where attention drops, and cut thirty more seconds from there.

Avoid

Do not speed up or quick-cut your way through it; repeated information does not disappear when cut faster.

Boundary

This page only covers “The Film Is Too Long”; it does not replace The Transition Is Jarring, Wind Noise Outside, 16mm: Cinema Left the Theater. When the problem belongs to one of those pages, use that page instead of adding more parameters here.

Alternative

If “Label what new information each scene provides; cut the scenes that provide none” is not the main problem, start with “Merge characters and locations that do the same job”; only upgrade after both hold.

60-second test

Run one 60-second test against check 1: Every scene is labeled with its new information; scenes without any are cut. If check 2 (Characters and locations that do the same job are merged) does not change, do not add gear or budget.

Check

  • Every scene is labeled with its new information; scenes without any are cut.
  • Characters and locations that do the same job are merged.
  • Thirty more seconds came out after the test-screening attention dip.

Deep dive

About 8 minutes. This section does not repeat the method above. It adds three things: two conditions, a counterexample and a reusable model, so you can decide when to change the method.

Condition A: narrative short

A narrative short is usually too long not because shots are long, but because the change arrives late. Budget by act: first quarter setup, half development, quarter turn; each act needs a visible change. Without change, the audience feels slow even when shots are short.

Do not cut from the end first; cut the scene that repeats information. If two scenes both say “he is lonely”, remove the second; if two both say “the client is difficult”, remove the explanation. Keep change, remove repetition.

Condition B: platform version

A platform cut is too long because of the entry, not the story. The feed version removes setup and puts the person or problem in the first three seconds; the site version can keep setup but must give information in the first ten; the channel version follows the slot, not patience. Three lengths, not three stories.

Do not solve a platform cut with speed: 1.1x makes the audience breathless. Removing one complete passage works better than speeding the whole film.

Counterexample: trimming pauses, not scenes

A twelve-minute short needs to become eight. The editor trims one second from the head and tail of every shot and removes four minutes. It is shorter but still slow because two repeated scenes remain. The audience feels faster pacing but no movement.

The fix is not more trimming. Make a scene-information-change table: any scene with no new information goes entirely; any scene with change stays. Cutting scenes hurts, but it works better than speeding the film.

Reusable model

Length budget → scene table → remove repetition → test screening. Allocate acts by length, list each scene's information and change, remove repeats, then screen and watch where attention drops. The drop is not impatience; it is repeated information.

Remove one scene at a time and watch. Remove three and the audience loses the thread. The test is “does the rest still work without it?” If yes, cut; if no, keep.
