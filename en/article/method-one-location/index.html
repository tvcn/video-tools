You Only Have One Room

One room works if it changes once during the film: messier, emptier, darker.

3 min read · Field noteTVCN EditorialEdited 2026-11

  4. On set
  5. Do this
  6. Avoid

Break one room into six shots

  • Blocking: from door to table, table to window; a change in position is a change in scene.
  • Off-screen: sound, a phone, a neighbor, the corridor can double the room.
  • Inserts: hands, a cup, a screen, the gap under a door replace a new location.
  • Light: one lamp or one curtain changes the time of day in the same room.

A six-shot test

Shoot six different sizes and angles in one room without repeating a setup: wide, medium, close, low, high and an off-screen sound. Cut 30 seconds and see whether it tells a change. Do not hide flat blocking behind a new location.

When not to add a location

If the relationship is not established inside one room, a new location only adds cost. Stage the power relationship in the room first.

On set

Shoot the same room in three states: tidy, messy, empty. Change the space with light and props.

Do this

One room must change on screen: tidy to messy, day to dusk, full to empty. When the space changes, the story moves.

  1. Shoot three empty frames from the same camera position, noting light and props.
  2. Change the space with three actions: open a box, draw the curtain, move the chair away.
  3. Add one close-up detail per stage so the audience sees the change.

Avoid

Do not only change camera positions; the audience will feel the film is circling in place.

3 checks

  • Three empty frames from one position show light and prop changes.
  • Three actions leave the space in different states: full, messy, empty.
  • One close-up per stage proves the change.
