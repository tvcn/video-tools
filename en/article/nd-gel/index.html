Lighting Gel: CTO, CTB, ND

Know CTO, CTB, and ND: warm, cool, and dim. The wrong gel changes the whole set.

Test the color shift

  • Density: shoot white paper and a gray card at the final light position with 1/4, 1/2 and full ND; compare color temperature and density.
  • Stacking: multiple ND gels add cast and vignetting; use one gel of the needed density where possible.
  • Heat: gels melt too close to a fixture; use a gel frame and distance, and check LED cooling too.

When not to use it

For precise, repeatable light reduction, use lens ND or electronic ND, not a guess with a gel. Gels are for color and atmosphere, not measurement.

Do not stack more than two gels

CTO/CTB correct color temperature; ND lowers brightness. Stacking both shifts color, reduces sharpness and can add IR pollution. Decide whether you need a color shift before adding ND.

  1. Daylight too blue: 1/4 CTO. Tungsten too warm: 1/4 CTB. Add a quarter at a time.
  2. Put ND on the lens or window, not against the color gel. For deep cuts, use one strong ND, not three weak ones.
  3. Set LED color and brightness first; gels are for trim, not for fixing a bad lamp.

Acceptance: white-balance and check white paper and skin, not the lamp. Green cast or vignette means remove a layer.

On set

Put CTO on the window and CTB on the lamp, then check the color temperature. ND gel only cuts brightness; it does not shift color.

Do this

Lighting gels split into CTO, CTB, and ND: warm, cool, and dim; the wrong gel changes the whole set.

  1. CTO on a window, CTB on a lamp, shoot and read color temperature.
  2. ND gel only dims without shifting color.
  3. Label each gel with name and strength instead of judging by color.

Avoid

Do not sort gels by eye; CTO and ND can both look orange in the dark.

3 checks

  • CTO and CTB each have a comparable color-temperature sample.
  • ND changes brightness without shifting color temperature.
  • Each gel carries a name and strength label.
