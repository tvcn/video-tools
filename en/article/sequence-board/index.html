Sequence Board: Shoot the Scene, Not the Script

A sequence board does not illustrate every line; it decides what the audience sees first.

A sequence board is not a spread of beautiful panels. First decide how the previous shot ends and how the next begins. Each cut names the match—action, gaze or sound—then the panels fill in.

Lay out the cuts before drawing panels

Use a landscape sheet: shot number on the left, cut logic on the right. If two panels do not connect, fix the cut before the drawing.

Limits

Panels do not need finish, but each must answer why it sits here rather than before or after.

On set

Draw three panels for one scene: what the audience sees first, what is hidden, what is revealed last. Then fill the middle; do not illustrate every line.

Do this

  1. Draw three panels: what to show first, what to hide, what to reveal last.
  2. Fill the middle shots so information releases in order.
  3. Board information, not one panel per line.

Avoid

Do not draw one panel per line; a board is information order, not illustration.

Judge

Three signals are observable on set:

  • the want is visible in one action
  • the obstacle escalates inside the same scene
  • delete one expository line and the conflict still holds

Failures

These three moves erase the detail you just built:

  • the want is unclear and the scene is only mood
  • the obstacle is solved by coincidence, not a decision
  • dialogue repeats what the image already says

Extend

10-minute exercise: Write one 30-second scene with only “Draw three panels: what to show first, what” and “Fill the middle shots so information releases in”. Cut every explanation and ask one person to retell what the character wants.

3 checks

  • The first three panels show what is shown, hidden, and revealed last.
  • Middle shots fill by information order, not line count.
  • A third person can repeat the release order from the board.
