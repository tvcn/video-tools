Composition: Tell the Eye Where to Look

Who should the eye hit first, and where does it go second? Composition is that order. Set the subject, then the layers.

One frame, one subject

The eye lands in one place first. Use the brightest, sharpest, most central element to say who matters; everything else supports it.

If the face and the background fight, darken the background, move the clutter, or step closer. Composition is subtraction first.

Foreground, middle, background

Foreground sets space, middle ground holds the person, background carries information. All three layers give depth; if all three compete, the frame becomes noise.

With no foreground, find a leaf, a door frame, a shoulder. It does not need to matter; it only needs to separate the space.

Leave room for gaze and movement

If the person looks right, leave space on the right; if they walk left, leave a path on the left. Negative space is not waste; it lets the audience anticipate the next second.

Landscape and vertical need different lead room. In vertical, keep the face in the safe area first, then talk about side space.

Three checks

  • Pause the frame; you can name the subject in three seconds.
  • The frame has at least two layers of space.
  • There is lead room in the direction of the gaze.

Deep dive

About 8 minutes. This section does not repeat the method above. It adds three things: two conditions, a counterexample and a reusable model, so you can decide when to change the method.

Condition A: wide or environmental composition

Wide composition is not “fit everything in”; it is “show where the person is in the space”. Set three lines first: horizon, wall line and eyeline; do not let them fight. Placing the person on a third is not a rule; it gives the space room to breathe.

Wide lenses distort edges; keep important faces inside the central sixty percent and leave the edge to environment. The audience looks at the face, then the space; reverse the order and the person becomes a dot in a landscape.

Condition B: close or two-shot composition

Close composition starts with where the eyes sit, then where the shoulders are cut. Do not cut on a joint; cut at the chest or upper arm. Put the eyes on the upper third and leave room under the chin. In a two-shot, do not make both people the same size unless you mean “they are equal”; one larger gives the relationship a direction.

Simplify the close background, do not just blur it. Blur hides things and information; if a detail matters, keep it instead of wiping it with a wide aperture.

Counterexample: correct composition, nobody looks

A frame follows the rule of thirds, the person sits on a power point, the background has leading lines and the colours match. But the audience looks at the lamp in the background, not the person, because the person has no action and the background is brighter.

The fix is not moving the person to the centre; give them an action and bring the background down a stop. Composition ends at “the audience knows where to look”, not “the rules are met”. Ask who is brightest, who moves and who has eyes; if the three answers differ, the frame is scattered.

Reusable model

Eyes → weight → edges → background. Set where the person looks, then visual weight, then check edge distractions, then decide what the background keeps. Follow the order; do not start with the rule of thirds.

After the take, watch three seconds on monitor: where the eye lands first, what it sees second, whether a third thing distracts. If three seconds cannot answer, move the camera, not the lens.
