14-Day Live-Action Short

Take a 3–8 minute live-action short from scope to delivery in fourteen days with two to four people, one camera, and one mic.

Before you start

Confirm a crew of two to four for two weeks, a camera that shoots 4K or 1080p, one working mic, one controllable location, and a backup for cast and location.

How the path runs

Fourteen days split into six stages: the first eight are paper and prep, days nine to twelve shoot, the last two are post and delivery. Do not move on without the stage deliverable.

  1. Days 1–2: Set scope: Write who wants what and what blocks them. List three things you cannot afford: crowds, children, animals, rain at night, underwater, multiple locations. Delete three. Set a three-to-eight minute target; if it runs longer, cut scenes before cutting sound. Output: One-line story, three limits, runtime target.
  2. Days 3–4: Script and board: One change per scene: who gains or loses what. Board action before framing; one main action per shot. Draw a camera plan per scene with the axis, window light, and a backup position. Output: Three to five scenes, twelve to twenty shots, one camera plan per scene.
  3. Days 5–6: Crew, location, permits: Confirm cast to the hour, not just the day. Confirm load-in, noise limits, load-out, and the nearest hospital. Ask for permits before shooting in public spaces, shops, transit, or parks. Every critical resource gets a backup and a contact. Output: Cast and location confirmations, permit list, backup contacts.
  4. Days 7–8: Budget and call sheet: Split the budget into people, gear, location, post, and contingency; contingency at least ten percent. Build the schedule backward from the shoot days: fittings, tech scout, and recce. The call sheet lists time, address, contact, parking, meals, nearest hospital, and load-out; send it a day early. Output: A five-block budget, a reverse schedule, a call sheet.
  5. Days 9–12: Shoot: Start each day with a wide and a master so the edit has coverage. On every new setup, check axis and light continuity. Record thirty seconds of room tone before dialogue scenes. Back up and reconcile the shot log before wrap; do not fix yesterday's gap today. Output: Daily media backup, shot log, and a safe master.
  6. Days 13–14: Post and delivery: Cut structure, then clean dialogue, then grade; do not polish effects while the cut is moving. Lock one master: resolution, bitrate, subtitles, loudness, and filename on one delivery sheet. Check the export on a computer, a phone, and a speaker. Output: A finished film, a delivery sheet, a project backup.

Three exercises

  1. Exercise 1: Scout twice: Visit the same location at midday and dusk. Take three photos and write three risks: noise, neighbors, load-out. Output: Three scout photos and three risks.
  2. Exercise 2: One-page call sheet: Write one page for a six-person crew: time, address, contact, parking, meals, hospital, load-out. Output: A call sheet ready to send.
  3. Exercise 3: Three-minute assembly: Cut three minutes from existing footage, keeping only story-moving shots; no music yet. Output: A three-minute v1.

Deliverables

  • One-line story and three limits
  • Three to five scenes and twelve to twenty shots
  • Cast/location confirmations and permit list
  • Five-block budget, reverse schedule, call sheet
  • Daily backups and shot logs
  • A finished film, delivery sheet, and project backup

Where it goes wrong

  • Treating a full pipeline as a big crew: two people can finish if every stage has a deliverable.
  • Shooting an unlocked script: every on-set rewrite resets the board, call sheet, and budget.
  • Skipping backup: one lost card erases the fourteen days.

3 checks

  • Fourteen days split into 3, 6, 5 with matching dates.
  • Day seven holds a half-day for backup and pickups.
  • Each phase ends with a playable file as its deliverable.
