Matte Box: Filters and Stray Light

A matte box cuts stray light before holding filters; a cheap one can leak and ruin the frame.

3 min read · Field noteTVCN Tools DeskEdited 2026-11

  2. When not to use it

  5. Avoid
  6. 3 checks

For light travel, wide lenses or fast lens changes, a matte box adds bulk and steps; use a lens hood and light filters. Do not trade mobility for “professional look.”

What to compare first

  • Filter stages: how many slots, rotating polarizer and ND support; stage order affects vignetting.
  • Diameter and shading: a deep matte box clips wide lenses; test the widest focal length first.
  • Mount and light leaks: flags and top brow must lock; leaks show in long exposures and backlight.

When not to use it

On set

Shoot black velvet against the light and check for leaks at the matte box edge. Add a filter and rotate it to check vignetting.

Do this

A matte box blocks stray light before holding filters; a cheap one can leak and ruin the image.

  1. Shoot black velvet in backlight and check the matte box edges for leaks.
  2. Rotate the filter and check for vignetting.
  3. Seal leaks with black cloth or tape.

Avoid

Do not treat a matte box as a filter holder; check leaks before loading filters.

3 checks

  • A backlit black velvet test shows whether edges leak.
  • Filter rotation logs where vignetting appears.
  • Leaks are sealed with black cloth or tape, not ignored.
