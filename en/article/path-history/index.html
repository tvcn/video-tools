Pick four works: a nineteenth-century photograph, a 1920s silent film, a 1950s TV segment, and a 2020s generated video. Watch five minutes each, no notes.

Before you start

How the path runs

No date memorization. Each node answers two questions: what old method did it replace, and what new limit did it add?

  1. 1839: Photography makes reality copyable: Photography brought a claim of evidence: someone can say “this was there then.” It also brought new problems: exposure time, framing, who is photographed, who is absent. Ask who stood before the camera, then who did not. Output: One note on what photography replaced.
  2. 1895: Cinema starts charging: Cinema turned images from solo viewing into a public event: screen, seats, darkness, collective reaction. Editing followed because an audience had to follow multiple places within an hour. Film history is the history of how audiences are organized, not just gear. Output: One note on what public viewing changed.
  3. 1927: Sound enters the frame: Synchronized dialogue stopped actors from explaining everything with the body, and cuts could not be arbitrary because sound had to continue. Recording gear constrained camera positions and forced new blocking. Technology rewrote performance and editing. Output: Two comparison notes: performance and editing.
  4. 1950s: TV moves cinema home: Smaller screens brought viewers closer and pushed framing closer. Slots, ads, and seasons cut content into fixed durations. TV was not “small cinema”; it invented its own time units: thirty minutes, sixty seconds, the same hour every week. Output: One note on TV's time units.
  5. 2000s: Digital cuts the cost: Digital shooting made takes cheap, editing moved from suites to laptops, and distribution moved from cinemas and TV to the web. Cost did not vanish; it moved to storage, backup, version control, and attention. Digital is not free; the bill moved. Output: An old-cost/new-cost table.
  6. 2020s: Images start being generated: Generation turns shooting into sampling: a shot need not exist first; it can be described. The old problems were sets, cast, weather, and budget. The new ones are consistency, provenance, rights, disclosure, and whether the shot ever happened. The more it looks real, the harder evidence becomes. Output: One note on what generation adds.

Three exercises

  1. Exercise 1: Four timelines on one page: Draw four lines: photography, cinema, sound/TV, digital/generation; five nodes each. Output: One page of timelines.
  2. Exercise 2: Before and after one work: Pick one work and write how people solved the same problem before and after it. Output: Two paragraphs, two hundred characters each.
  3. Exercise 3: Three limits of a technology: Pick a technology you use and write three things it cannot do. Output: Three limits.

Deliverables

  • One page of four timelines
  • Three work analyses
  • One old-problem/new-problem table
  • One technology-limit list

Where it goes wrong

  • Memorizing a timeline: forgetting years is fine; naming what replaced what is not.
  • Only directors and masterpieces: technical history is also workers, audiences, distribution, and censorship.
  • Technological determinism: technology offers possibilities; people decide use and exclusion.
