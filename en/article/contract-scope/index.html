Contract Scope: Price the “One More Revision” Before It Happens

The most common film contract dispute is not payment; it is scope. Unclear deliverables, revisions, rights, acceptance, and termination become unpaid overtime.

Scope

This page covers general commercial film contracts in China mainland; it is not legal advice. Have a lawyer review the actual client, platform, and subject. Minors, likeness, locations, and music need separate clearances.

Key terms: eight must-writes

1. Deliverables: count, duration, ratio, subtitles, cover, vertical/horizontal, project files. Do not write “one video.” 2. Revisions: rounds, scope per round, overage fee, client feedback window. 3. Acceptance: script/storyboard/review sheet as the standard; days deemed approved. 4. Rights: footage, music, fonts, AI-generated content; whether the client can recut or run paid ads. 5. Payment: deposit, start, first cut, final; late interest. 6. Termination: client cancel, force majeure, delay settlement. 7. Liability: late delivery, non-payment, out-of-scope use. 8. Confidentiality and credit: NDA, portfolio use, credit form.

Process: from quote to change order

Write a one-page Scope of Work before quoting; every line item maps to a deliverable. Hold a kickoff and name one approver. Use one revision sheet, marking “in scope/out of scope.” Issue a Change Order for out-of-scope work with cost and delay; get written approval before doing it. Check the delivery list before final; do not let “one more vertical cut” become a free default.

Common disputes: three failure modes

“It feels off”: no review sheet, subjective reversals. Fix: script, reference, storyboard, and sample as stage gates. “Just a small change”: a structural change is a re-edit/re-shoot. Fix: price structural changes as a new version. “Launch first, pay later”: platform schedule forces the producer to front costs. Fix: payment before final, or watermarked low-res until acceptance.

Template: one-page Scope of Work

Project/client/producer/version date; objective and audience; deliverable table (count, duration, ratio, language, subtitles, cover, project files); stages and review gates; revision rounds and overage rate; footage/music rights; payment milestones; termination and force majeure; signatures. Attachments: references, storyboard, review sheet, asset list.

Regional note for US / UK / EU readers

3 checks

  • Deliverables specify length, resolution, format, and date.
  • Revision rounds and scope have numbers.
  • Copyright, usage, and payment milestones are each verifiable.

Go deeper: for people already shooting

A contract is not distrust. It writes down what is made, for how long, where it is used, and how many revisions.

One step past the field note

Deliverables you can count

Do not write “a brand video.” Write “one sixty-second master, three fifteen-second cutdowns, one cover, two subtitle versions.” Count, duration, ratio, format.

Delivery dates are dates, not “as soon as possible.” Review rounds are numbers, not “until satisfied.” A clause you can count is a clause you can execute.

Rights more detailed than price

Media, term, territory, exclusivity, sublicense: five separate lines. Organic usage is not paid media; one year is not forever.

State whether source files are delivered, when, and which layers. Many disputes are not about money; the client thinks they bought everything, you think they bought the finished film.

Termination and surprises

Write cancellation milestones, costs incurred, whether the deposit is refundable, footage ownership, and force majeure. Do not only write “both parties will negotiate.” Negotiation is a process; clauses are the floor.

If client approval slips beyond a set number of days, the schedule moves. If the direction changes, it is a new quote. Two lines save many arguments.

Five-minute drill: four-line contract summary

Reduce a one-page contract to four lines: what, when, how many revisions, which rights. Twenty words per line, readable by the client and executable by you.

If one line is vague, rewrite the contract. Do not fill it with “we will see.” What is not in the contract will not exist in delivery.

When the contract is clear

The contract is clear when both sides can state the same four things without the appendix: deliverables, dates, revisions, rights. Four answers match.

If they differ, it is not bad memory; the contract allowed two readings. Rewrite it instead of explaining later.

Ten contract-scope checks

  • Count, duration, ratio of deliverables
  • Delivery dates as dates
  • Review rounds as numbers
  • Media on its own line
  • Term on its own line
  • Territory on its own line
  • Exclusivity and sublicense separate
  • Source-file delivery separate
  • Cancellation and schedule slip written
  • Both sides can repeat the same four

A contract's value is not who you can sue. It is giving two people the same definition before there is a problem.
