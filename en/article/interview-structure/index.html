Interview Structure: The Question Matters More

The question matters more than the camera; a vague question gets a vague answer.

Shoot the work before the talk

The worst interview opens on a face reciting a résumé. Let the person complete one action in the real workplace so the audience sees the difficulty, then sit down and ask why that step is done that way.

Questions circle the action, not a life story. Three are enough: what is hardest here, what failed before, what changes next time.

Limits

Do not cut the interview into quote confetti. Every line keeps its context; removing the question and keeping the answer can change the meaning.

Case: a five-minute interview that replaced the bio with a failure

Case note: an anonymised composite of several similar projects. Figures are ranges; people, places and clients are withheld. It does not represent one real client job.

Five-minute portrait interviewOne founder, one afternoon, two camerasThree questions carry the film
Budget band¥3,000–8,000Office daylight + one fill
PurposeRemember one decisionNot a company CV

What happened

  1. The first 20 minutes asked for a company introduction; every answer was a pitch. We stopped and asked, “What was the last decision you got wrong?”
  2. The subject began naming a person, a date and a loss. The camera did not cut; four seconds of silence stayed so viewers wait for the end of the sentence.
  3. All company facts moved to end cards. The interview kept one decision and its cost.

The trade-off

We dropped the full “founder story plus milestones” arc and built around one failure. The second camera did not shoot reactions; it shot hands and the desk, giving the edit detail. Music only in the first five seconds; the rest is voice.

Reusable / not transferable

  • Reusable: ask for a specific experience, not an identity; let the guest do or remember an action, not read a bio.
  • Not transferable: undisclosed business decisions need clearance before publishing. Do not force “authenticity” into something the guest cannot say.

On set

Ask a question that makes them do something: show me how you do it. Do not ask about feelings.

Do this

In an interview, the question matters more than the camera; an empty question yields an empty answer.

  1. Ask a question that makes them do something: show me how you work.
  2. Shoot the actions and pauses in the answer.
  3. Add one environment shot.

Avoid

Do not ask “how do you feel”; feelings cannot be shown, actions can.

3 checks

  • The first question makes the subject do something, not just answer.
  • Actions or pauses are captured within the answer.
  • An environment shot establishes where they are.

DEEP DIVE / 07 MIN

Go deeper: for people already shooting

An interview is not a recorded Q&A. Give the guest something to do and let the questions follow the action.

One step past the field note

Questions that produce action

Do not ask “how did you feel?” Ask “what did you do first that day?” Do not ask “why did you persist?” Ask “when did you almost quit, and what happened the last time?”

Follow the detail: what time, where, who was there, what did you say, how did they answer. Detail brings emotion with it; do not force the guest to perform it.

The scene

Interview at the place where they work: hands busy, eyes not locked on the lens. Cooking, repairing, sorting papers beats a white wall.

Two cameras are nice; one works too: a medium and a hand or room detail. Without a second camera, shoot hand close-ups after the Q&A. Record thirty seconds of room tone separately.

Structure

One question, one story, one contradiction, one close. Do not run from childhood to today. Let the audience hear one concrete scene first, then add context.

Do not narrate “he is persistent.” Let the guest name the cost. Subtitles must be accurate; keep pauses and repetition where they carry meaning, do not rewrite everything into formal prose.

Five-minute drill: three action questions

Ask one person three questions only: what did you do first that day, who stopped you, what did you change in the end? No feelings, no “what do you think.”

Let them answer while doing something with their hands. Record sixty seconds and look for one concrete scene. If there is none, follow up: where were you, what time, who was nearby?

When it is done

Ask someone who has never met the guest to repeat one concrete scene: where they were, what their hands were doing, who blocked them. If they can, the interview is done. If all they retain is “he worked hard,” go back for action detail, not narration.

Ten interview checks

  • Questions ask actions, not feelings
  • At least three detail follow-ups
  • Guest is at the work site
  • Hands have something to do
  • Hand close-ups even with one camera
  • Thirty seconds of room tone
  • Concrete scene before context
  • One contradiction
  • No narration summarizing the person
  • Subtitles keep spoken texture

An interview does not get answers; it lets the viewer see the part of the person that appears while they work.
